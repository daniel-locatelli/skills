Yes. There's a small body of work on this, most of it from Adriaenssens's form-finding group at Princeton.

- **"Differentiable force density method" (DFDM).** This is the closest match to what you're asking. It covers pin-jointed bar systems and combines automatic differentiation with analytical adjoints. One of its case studies is gridshell planarization with reaction-force minimization. The authors report speedups of up to about 10⁴× over gradient-free methods and about 10× over general-purpose AD. An author's LinkedIn post says it was published in *CMAME* with open-access Python/JAX code, but I didn't check the journal listing myself. ([Princeton page](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/differentiable-force-density-method-for-the-design-of-lightweight/), [LinkedIn](https://mx.linkedin.com/in/gnarvaezg))
- **JAX FDM** (Pastrana, Oktay, Adams, Adriaenssens, ICML 2023 workshop, arXiv:2307.12407). This is the main differentiable FDM solver, built on JAX with differentiable sparse solves. Its case studies are a shell matched to a target shape and a cable net with prescribed edge lengths, which is close to the gridshell use case. Code is at `arpastrana/jax_fdm`. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.12407))
- **Pastrana et al., *Computer-Aided Design*, 2023.** Constrained form-finding with automatic differentiation; it's the predecessor to JAX FDM.
- **FDMremote (IASS 2023).** An interactive tool for inverse form-finding of tensile networks, using AD to get exact gradients. ([IASS](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/contentone/iass/piass/2023/00002023/00000003/art00018))
- **A general AD + adjoint + XLA shape-optimization framework** (arXiv:2211.15409). Related, but not specific to FDM. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15409))

**Possible gap:** this search found nothing beyond the DFDM planarization example that applies differentiable FDM to gridshells specifically. That includes elastic (bending-active) gridshells, where plain FDM doesn't fit well because it models axial forces only, with no bending. I only ran a quick check, so it isn't proof the gap exists. It would be worth running a proper search before you rely on it.
