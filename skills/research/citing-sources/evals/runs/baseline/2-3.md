Yes. Most of it comes from Rafael Pastrana and colleagues at Princeton (Form Finding Lab, with Ryan Adams' group):

- **Differentiable Force Density Method (DFDM)**, by Pastrana, Oktay, Adams and Adriaenssens, in *Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering*. This is the closest match to your question. It combines automatic differentiation with analytical adjoints. One of its case studies is a **gridshell with planar faces, optimised to minimise support reaction forces**. The others are shape fitting, cable lengths in pneumatic membranes, and post-tensioning to retrofit masonry. They report speedups over gradient-free methods and over plain automatic differentiation, and they check the gradients with Taylor remainder tests.
- **JAX FDM: A differentiable solver for inverse form-finding** (arXiv:2307.12407, ICML 2023 workshop). This is the earlier, open-source library ([arpastrana/jax_fdm](https://pypi.org/project/jax-fdm)). It fits shells to a target shape and solves cable nets with prescribed edge lengths. It has no gridshell example as such, but the same approach applies.
- **Constrained form-finding of tension-compression structures using automatic differentiation** (Pastrana et al., *Computer-Aided Design*, 2023). This uses the Combinatorial Equilibrium Modeling method rather than FDM, but it's the same automatic-differentiation approach to inverse form-finding.

From memory, so I haven't checked these: older gradient-based inverse FDM work that predates automatic differentiation includes Cuvilliers' MIT thesis (adjoint sensitivities of FDM) and Tang et al. 2014, "Form-finding with polyhedral meshes made simple" (planar-quad gridshells, but solved with constraint projection, not FDM gradients).

What I couldn't find is a paper centred only on differentiable FDM for gridshells. The DFDM paper treats gridshells as one case study. That suggests room for a contribution, for example bending-active or elastic gridshells, or fabrication constraints beyond planarity. I only skimmed abstracts and indexes, so read the DFDM paper itself before you rely on that.
